# Positioning: A Falsifiable Claim, Not a Slogan

Positioning is the answer to three questions asked together: **who is this for, what does
it replace, and what is the one attribute that makes it worth switching?** Get it right
and every downstream decision — the landing page, the demo, the first ten customers, what
to build next — gets cheaper. Get it vague and the product tries to be for everyone and
lands with no one.

This skill backs `sg_draft_positioning`. It **drafts** a positioning statement; it does
not decide it. Positioning is a bet about the market that reaches customers, so it is a
founder call under the methodology's standing division — the agent frames it, a human
disposes. See the RECOMMEND phase in
[`startup-generalist-methodology`](./SKILL.md).

## The three questions positioning must answer

- **Who is it for.** A specific, addressable best-fit segment — not "teams" or
  "developers" but the group for whom the unique attribute is decisive. If the answer is
  "everyone," there is no positioning yet.
- **What it replaces.** Positioning is always relative to a **competitive alternative** —
  and the alternative is often a spreadsheet, a manual process, or doing nothing, not a
  named competitor. A product with no alternative is a product no one is currently solving
  the problem some other way, which usually means the problem is not felt.
- **The one attribute that matters.** The single unique capability that the alternative
  lacks and the best-fit segment cares about. One. A list of five "differentiators" is a
  confession that none of them is decisive.

## The scaffold

Build the statement in this order — each slot constrains the next:

```
competitive alternative  →  unique attribute  →  value  →  best-fit segment

  what they use today        the one thing         the concrete outcome    who feels that
  (or "nothing")             it does that the       the attribute           value most
                             alternative can't      produces                acutely
```

Read left to right it is a chain of because: *for* the best-fit segment, *who* find the
competitive alternative wanting in one specific way, this product provides the unique
attribute, *which delivers* the value. Every link must hold. A unique attribute that
produces no value the segment cares about is a feature, not a position; a value with no
attribute behind it is a wish.

## Make the claim falsifiable

A positioning statement is a hypothesis about the market, and a hypothesis you cannot
disprove teaches you nothing. Aspirational positioning — "the easiest way to X," "the
platform for Y" — cannot be tested and so cannot be wrong, which is exactly why it is
worthless. Draft it so it *could* fail:

- The best-fit segment is **nameable and reachable** — you can point at who to go ask.
- The unique attribute is **checkable** — the alternative genuinely lacks it, and that is
  a fact about the world, not a phrasing.
- The value is **something a user would confirm or deny** after using the product, not a
  mood.

A good positioning claim comes with the test attached: *if the best-fit segment does not
find the unique attribute decisive, this positioning is wrong.* State that test alongside
the statement.

## No invented proof, no invented differentiators

This is where positioning work goes bad, and it goes bad quietly. The temptation is to
fill the scaffold with what would sound good rather than what is true.

- **Do not invent a differentiator.** If you cannot name the one attribute the alternative
  genuinely lacks, the empty slot is the finding — surface it, do not paper over it with a
  generic claim ("faster," "simpler," "AI-powered") that the alternative could say too.
- **Do not invent proof.** Every value claim needs something behind it — a measured
  outcome, a user who said it, a mechanism that makes it true. An **unproven value claim is
  a flagged slot**, not a sentence to ship. Mark it: "asserted, not yet evidenced."
- **Flag every empty slot.** No known best-fit segment, no proven attribute, no evidenced
  value — say so explicitly. A positioning statement with three honest gaps is more useful
  than a polished one with three hidden fabrications, because the founder can act on the
  gaps and cannot act on the fabrications until they blow up in a sales call.

Per the methodology's no-fabrication rule: an empty slot is information. Fill it with a
guess and you have converted a known gap into a claim the venture will defend to customers.

## Frame it for the founder to decide and to test

The terminal output is a **drafted** positioning statement plus the seams:

- The statement, in the scaffold's order, with each slot filled or explicitly flagged
- The competitive alternative it is drawn against, named
- Which value claims are evidenced and which are asserted
- The falsification test — who to ask, and what answer would sink it
- The empty slots, listed, with what it would take to fill each

Then hand it over. The founder decides whether to adopt it, and — because it is
falsifiable — takes it to the best-fit segment and finds out. The agent's job is done when
the founder has a claim sharp enough to be wrong and a way to check it; deciding it, and
testing it against real customers, is theirs.
